# Draft — Discussion

The results demonstrate a substantial gap between in-domain PPE detection performance and cross-domain safety reliability. Although YOLO11m retained moderate aggregate in-domain performance on both harmonized datasets, transfer to the alternate domain produced an approximately 0.17 absolute loss in mAP50:95 in both directions. Importantly, this degradation was stable across three independent seeds, indicating that the effect reflects a persistent domain-generalization limitation rather than stochastic training variation.

The safety implications are more severe than the aggregate AP values alone suggest. The violation classes no_helmet, no_gloves, and no_boots exhibited very high false-negative rates, and the deterministic consensus analysis showed that most violation instances were missed by all three models in each transfer direction. In particular, no_boots reached a 100% consensus false-negative rate in both directions. For an automated safety-monitoring system, such failures are operationally critical because a model may appear acceptable under aggregate object-detection metrics while systematically failing to identify the very non-compliance events that motivate deployment.

The dataset audit provides evidence for several plausible contributors but does not justify a single causal explanation. Construction-PPE showed stronger class imbalance and substantially lower no_boots support than Y-PPE, while the two domains also differed in object-scale distributions. These factors are consistent with the observed failures. However, they are insufficient as complete explanations. Construction-PPE no_helmet had substantially more training instances than Y-PPE no_helmet yet still produced near-floor violation performance, and cross-domain consensus misses in Construction→Y occurred even for medium and large safety objects. The failure therefore appears broader than a simple rare-class or small-object problem and may involve domain-specific appearance, contextual cues, annotation semantics, or learned correlations that do not transfer between datasets.

A key methodological implication is that single-dataset validation can overstate deployment readiness. Models trained and evaluated within the same curated domain may learn dataset-specific regularities that are not preserved across construction environments. For safety-critical applications, evaluation protocols should therefore include external-domain testing, multiple random seeds, class-specific violation metrics, and false-negative-oriented analysis rather than relying only on aggregate mAP.

The study also has clear limitations. Only one detector architecture is included in the revised analysis because the originally planned RF-DETR training matrix was discontinued after incomplete runs; therefore, the present evidence supports conclusions about cross-domain reliability under the completed YOLO11m experiments rather than architecture-level superiority. The analysis uses two harmonized datasets and three seeds, which is sufficient to demonstrate reproducible degradation but not to estimate generalization across the full diversity of construction environments. In addition, the diagnostic audit identifies associations with imbalance and scale but does not experimentally isolate causal mechanisms. Future work should test domain adaptation, balanced sampling, safety-class reweighting, scale-aware augmentation, and multi-domain training under a preregistered external evaluation protocol.

Taken together, the results support a deployment-oriented conclusion: strong in-domain detection is not evidence of cross-site safety reliability. In resource-constrained construction settings, where environmental variability and limited opportunities for site-specific retraining are common, deployment decisions should be conditioned on demonstrated cross-domain recall for safety-critical violations rather than aggregate in-domain detection accuracy alone.
